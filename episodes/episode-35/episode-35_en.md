# Episode 35: Design for People Who Cannot Use It

[← Top / README](../../README.md)

Episode 35 is written as the episode where, even as the expansion of support becomes visible, the difficulty of making that support usable by anyone comes to the front.  
This time, Yuto notices the barriers created by applications and logs, and proposes to the system side a design that assumes people who cannot use the support.

---

## Main Text

Wednesday.  
Soma Yuto sat on a bench in the station square, watching the flow of people in the morning.

People commuting to work.  
People heading out to shop.  
People waiting for the bus.  
Someone pushing a stroller.  
An elderly person with a cane.  
Someone who wanted to sit and rest.  
Someone standing and looking at a smartphone.

The more the discussion of support expanded, the more the thing that bothered him most became the existence of people who were technically within the scope of support, yet could not use it.

The exchange he had with WindArc the day before was still in his head.

“The more support expands, the darker the shadows of the places it still cannot reach become.”

“Yeah. That really is it…”

A new notification arrived on his phone.

“Request for opinions from the Cooling Commons design team:  
Regarding support design based on the assumption of people who cannot use it.  
We are seeking proposals that take into account:  
・people who have difficulty submitting logs  
・people who have difficulty applying  
・people who have difficulty moving  
・people who are not used to systems like this.”

“…Finally.”

Up through Episode 34, they had talked again and again about expansion of support and the people left out by it.  
Beyond that point, it seemed they had finally reached the stage of placing “people who cannot use it” at the starting point.

Yuto opened a memo on the spot.

“Support design based on people who cannot use it: draft

1. People who have difficulty submitting logs  
・Increase automatic judgment  
・Use support tickets that are completed on site  
・Do not rely on writing everything later; make the support valid at the moment it is used

2. People who have difficulty applying  
・Distribute application-free ‘place cards’  
・Create paths that let anyone enter temporary cooling spaces in shopping streets, station areas, libraries, city offices, and similar places  
・Prepare a mechanism that family members or supporters can use on someone’s behalf

3. People who have difficulty moving  
・Increase cooling bases usable at the neighborhood level  
・Test visit-based collection of cooling logs  
・Coordinate with municipal mobility support for people who cannot go outside

4. People who are not used to systems  
・Make explanations short, gentle, and not limited to text  
・Use voice guidance and diagrams  
・Create a structure where the player community can help explain things.”

“…This should have been necessary from the beginning.”

Across the station square, the breeze carried a little of the mist.  
It was cool.  
But not everyone could notice it and stop there.

Yuto reviewed the memo slightly, then began entering his response into the opinion form for the design team.

“Response:

If support is to be designed with ‘people who cannot use it’ in mind, I think support should first move away from being something people apply for and toward being something placed where it is used.

The reasons are:
・to avoid a system where only people who can submit logs or applications benefit  
・to avoid leaving behind people who cannot move, people who find it hard to read explanations, and people pressed for time  
・to avoid making support something that reaches only people with the ability to access it

Specifically, I would like you to prioritize:
・application-free place-based support  
・rest support completed on site  
・cooling tickets usable by proxies  
・simplified explanations and voice support

Also, support needs to reach not only electricity bills or workplaces, but also moments in transit, waiting time, and days when someone feels unwell.
I think this kind of support is not only for ‘people who cannot use it,’ but also leads to design that is easier for everyone to use.”

After pressing the send button, some of the tension left his shoulders.

At that moment, an elderly woman sitting beside him glanced at the plate on the bench.

“Take a breather here.”

Seeing those small words, the woman smiled faintly and said,

“So it’s all right to sit here.”

Yuto gave a reflexive nod.

“Yes.  
Please use it as a place to rest for a while.”

“That helps.  
But it would be nice if you could understand that without having to read anything.”

Those words made Yuto look up sharply.

“Understand without having to read.”  
That might be the core of support design.

“You’re right.  
Support should be something people can notice by using it, not something that makes them read first.”

“Exactly.  
When difficult things are written there, that alone feels a little tiring.”

The woman said that and leaned back against the bench.

Looking at her back, Yuto thought:

“Designing for people who cannot use it  
is ultimately the same as getting closer to something anyone can use.”

In the afternoon, a reply came from WindArc.

“WindArc:  
Your opinion is very good.  
Especially the phrase ‘not something people apply for, but something placed where it is used’—  
I want to pass that on to the design team as it is.”

“If they can use it as-is, I’m grateful.”

“WindArc:  
Also, at the next design meeting,  
‘design for people who cannot use it’ has been placed at the very top of the agenda.”

“Oh, that far.”

“WindArc:  
Yes.  
The flow is shifting away from ‘support people who can use it first,’  
and toward ‘start by thinking about how it reaches people who cannot use it.’”

“…So it finally got that far.”

Yuto looked at his phone and smiled a little.

Support had revealed shadows as it expanded.  
Beyond seeing those shadows, the discussion of “assuming people who cannot use it” had finally begun to move.

In the evening, he returned to his apartment and opened his notebook.

“Today’s events  
・Submitted a proposal to the Cooling Commons design team’s opinion call for support design based on people who cannot use it  
　→Made people who have difficulty submitting logs, applying, moving, or understanding systems part of the premise  
　→Proposed application-free place-based support, rest support completed on site, cooling tickets usable by proxies, voice support, and related measures  
・At the station-front bench, an elderly woman said, ‘It would be nice if you could understand without having to read’  
　→Confirmed again that support depends more on ease of use than on difficult explanations  
・WindArc shared that ‘design for people who cannot use it’ would be placed as the top agenda item at the design meeting.”

“Today’s realization  
・Designing based on people who cannot use it is the same as creating support anyone can use  
・If support is built to reach only people with enough ability, it creates another kind of gap  
・Lowering the barriers of explanation and application leads to the true expansion of support.”

Finally, one line.

“Today’s line  
・Support becomes far gentler when it is designed backward from people who cannot use it, rather than adjusted to people who already can.”

When he checked his phone, his Cooling Credit had increased only slightly.

“Earned today: 0.010 CC  
Total: 0.723 CC”

“The number is plain.  
But the design side took a real step forward today.”

The world was still hot.  
But within that heat, Yuto had finally managed to place design based on “people who cannot use it” onto the table of the system.

## Afterword

Thank you for reading Episode 35.

This time, the theme was “design based on people who cannot use it,” as the next challenge after the expansion of support.  
What mattered here was thinking from the beginning not only about people who can submit applications or logs, but also people who cannot move easily, people who find reading difficult, and people who are not used to systems.

Yuto proposes application-free place-based support, rest support completed on site, cooling tickets usable by proxies, voice support, and related measures, while WindArc creates the path to bring that proposal into the design meeting.  
The elderly woman’s line at the bench—“It would be nice if you could understand without having to read”—also reinforces the idea that support should not be about explaining things in a difficult way, but about making them easy to notice by using them.

In Part 3, we will continue following how this idea of designing from people who cannot use it is reflected in actual systems and field operations.  
I would be glad if you continue watching how Yuto moves closer to design that is gentle for everyone.
