# Episode 16: The Corporate Consultation Desk

[← Top / README](../../README.md)

In Episode 16, just as the activity around COOL STREET and COOL WATER begins to settle, Yuto receives his first consultation from the corporate side.

Rather than a small chain store connected to his living zone, a company in a slightly more distant area asks for cooling logs written from a resident's perspective.

---

Saturday.

Soma Yuto was having an unusually slow afternoon.

His convenience-store shift had ended in the morning.

The afternoon was free.

The town was as hot as ever, but for the past several days he had been walking around looking for new signs and plates. Today, he intended to rest his legs.

While drinking barley tea, he opened **ThermoBalance**.

“Cumulative Cooling Credit: 0.427 CC”

“0.5 is coming into view…”

The amount was still nowhere near enough to change his living expenses.

But after walking through his town again, the number of places where he could genuinely feel that he had helped cool them had grown considerably.

Then a new notification slid onto the screen.

“A consultation regarding cooperation on a corporate cooling project has arrived.  
Category: System and Project Cooperation”

“A company…?”

Yuto straightened slightly.

Until now, he had mainly dealt with the municipality, the shopping street, and environmental organizations.

His only real corporate connection had been the family restaurant where WindArc worked.

This felt slightly different.

He opened the details.

“Consulting organization: Operator of a suburban shopping center (Region: neighboring city)

Excerpt from consultation:  
‘We are considering a cooling project around our facility.  
We would like to refer to your movement-route and shade logs written from a resident's perspective.  
We request permission to view your public reports and logs,  
and, if possible, a brief comment from you.’”

“…Permission to view them? They're already public.”

Yuto smiled wryly.

His cooling reports had already been shared with nearby players.

But from the company's perspective, they probably wanted to say formally that they intended to cite them and wanted a direct acknowledgment.

“A suburban shopping center…”

A scene appeared faintly in his mind.

A huge parking lot.

Multiple stores.

Restaurants, a cinema complex, and variety shops.

Cool indoors, but a sea of heat outside.

“It feels like half a step outside my part-time-job living zone.”

WindArc's family restaurant had been a suburban chain store located on the extension of daily life.

This consultation came from a corporate facility still close to his living zone, but larger in scale.

“What kind of comment do they want?”

The message continued.

“‘In particular, we would appreciate any observations you have as a resident regarding:  
- Describing an easier route from the parking lot to the entrance  
- The relationship between bench and shade positions and people's movement  
- The process of turning a place where people merely endure the heat into a place where they can feel slightly more at ease.’”

“…This company understands.”

They did not merely want to lower temperature.

An easier route.

A space that made people feel more at ease.

Those were exactly the kinds of words Yuto had been writing in his logs.

“They really want the resident's perspective.”

He hesitated briefly over whether to reply.

In the end, he decided to accept.

“If I turn this down, there isn't much point to Part 2.”

System and Project Cooperation.

Cooling Commons.

Corporate projects.

He had entered the phase of testing how to engage with them while remaining a resident.

Yuto opened the reply field.

“Draft reply:  
‘You may refer to my reports and logs.  
Regarding comments, I will share what I have felt within the limits of my experience as a resident.’”

A few seconds after he pressed send, an acceptance notice returned.

The consultation did not immediately become a concrete project.

For now, the distance was limited to reading logs and receiving online comments.

“That feels about right.”

It began not with an on-site inspection or a contract, but with a consultation desk.

At this stage, he could preserve his sense of being a resident.

That afternoon, Yuto opened his notebook and began drafting comments for the company.

“Resident comments on the suburban shopping center (draft)

1) An easier route from the parking lot to the entrance  
- Rather than showing only the straight, shortest route, I would like routes with even a little shade or wind to be made visible.  
- For example, adding a small sign or a different surface color to the shaded path so people can tell, ‘This is the easier route,’ would significantly change how the space feels.

2) Bench and shade positions, and people's movement  
- If the way shade falls is matched with where people actually stop near the entrance and around benches at the edge of the parking lot, those places can become small areas of relief even on hot days.  
- In my town, simply moving a bench toward the shaded side based on resident logs increased shade usage.

3) From a space where people only endure heat to one where they can feel more at ease  
- It is not enough to add cooling equipment. Signs that clearly communicate, ‘You may stay here’ or ‘You may rest here for a moment,’ can greatly reduce psychological strain.  
- I feel that a situation where people merely endure heat is caused not only by insufficient cooling, but also by not knowing where they are allowed to stop.”

As he wrote, he noticed himself sitting slightly straighter.

“When it's for a company, my language naturally gets stiff…”

But these were still comments from a resident.

There was no need to fill them with technical terms.

“This tone is probably just barely something I can preserve.”

Yuto pasted the draft into the comment-submission screen in **ThermoBalance**, edited it slightly, and sent it.

A few minutes later, another notification arrived.

“Reply from the company:

‘Thank you for the detailed comments.  
The ideas of making easier routes visible and providing signs that say people are allowed to stay have been recognized by our internal design team as extremely important perspectives.  
When we experimentally adjust movement routes and bench positions at one facility,  
we would like to use your comments as reference material.’”

“…They really read it.”

Beyond the words were faces in an unseen meeting room.

Even so, his comments had reached them.

“So this is what a corporate consultation desk is.”

Cooling logs.

Daily movement routes.

Benches and shade.

Signs.

Until now, he had written about them as parts of his own town.

Now they were beginning to extend toward a facility slightly farther away.

That evening, Yuto took a small detour and stood at a bus stop heading toward the neighboring city.

The name of the shopping center that had contacted him appeared in small letters on the destination display.

“Someday, I may end up going there to see it.”

For now, his involvement existed only through screens and words.

But if the movement routes and bench positions changed, he would probably want to see them with his own eyes.

His phone vibrated.

“Cooling Commons trial log updated:  
Case: Suburban shopping-center route adjustment (planned)  
Referenced comment: Resident comment (Soma Yuto)”

“There are more and more things marked planned.”

The riverside entrance.

The shopping street.

The suburban chain store.

And now the shopping center.

The Cooling Commons page was gradually accumulating projects that began with resident logs.

Back at his apartment, he opened his notebook.

“Today's events:  
- Consultation from the corporate side regarding a cooling project (request for comments)  
  → Operator of a suburban shopping center  
  → Submitted resident comments regarding movement routes, shade, benches, and signs  
  → Scheduled to be treated as reference material for an experimental facility-route adjustment  
- The shopping-center case was added as planned to the Cooling Commons trial log”

“Today's observations:  
- Companies also want resident perspectives such as easier routes and signs saying people may stay  
- Daily-life cooling logs can extend beyond my own town  
- At the current distance — a comment-based consultation desk — there is still room to remain involved as a resident”

Finally, as usual, he added one line.

“Today's line:  
- It is not yet a contract or a formal project, but words from a resident have begun mixing into a company's design notes.”

He checked his cumulative Cooling Credit.

“Earned today: 0.024 CC  
Cumulative total: 0.451 CC”

“Only a little more to 0.5.”

The numbers, the town, and the system were all moving forward little by little.

“Before the corporate side of Part 2 really begins, I'm glad I can learn this degree of distance through consultation.”

Yuto closed the notebook and exhaled.

The world was still hot.

But in his town and just beyond it, the presence of a company willing to listen to the words of residents had unmistakably begun to appear.

Thank you for reading Episode 16.

This episode introduced the first consultation from the corporate side within the system arc: a request from the operator of a suburban shopping center for comments about movement routes, shade, benches, and signs.

Yuto responds entirely in the language of a resident. By keeping the relationship at the level of a comment-based consultation desk, he allows his words to enter corporate design notes while preserving his own position.

This stage carefully establishes the first form in which resident language enters corporate design before the story moves into full corporate projects, formal Cooling Commons rules, and questions of appropriation and return.

I hope you will continue following how far Yuto can remain a resident while becoming involved in a larger stage.
